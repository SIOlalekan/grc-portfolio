## 4. Data protection principles

Personal data must be:

| Principle (UK GDPR Art. 5) | Practical expectation |
|---|---|
| Lawful, fair and transparent | Identify a lawful basis. Do not use data in ways people would not reasonably expect. Provide privacy information (sections 5–6). |
| Purpose limitation | Collect for specified, legitimate purposes. Do not reuse incompatibly without a fresh assessment. |
| Data minimisation | Adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary (section 9). |
| Accuracy | Accurate and kept up to date where necessary (section 10). |
| Storage limitation | Kept no longer than necessary (section 11). |
| Integrity and confidentiality | Protected by appropriate security (section 12). |
| Accountability | Responsibility for compliance, and the ability to demonstrate it (section 3). |

## 5. Lawful basis

- Before processing, the relevant manager must identify and record **one or more** lawful bases under Art. 6:

| Basis | Use when (summary) |
|---|---|
| Consent | A person gives a freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of agreement. It must be easy to withdraw. |
| Contract | Processing is necessary for a contract with the individual, or to take pre-contract steps at their request. |
| Legal obligation | Processing is necessary to comply with UK law. |
| Vital interests | Processing is necessary to protect someone's life. This is rarely used. |
| Public task | Processing is necessary for a task in the public interest or official functions with a legal basis. Usually relevant to public bodies. |
| Legitimate interests | Processing is necessary for a legitimate interest, and a documented balancing test shows individuals' rights do not override it. Check the current position on "recognised legitimate interests" under the 2025 Act. |

- The basis chosen should reflect the true relationship with the individual. It must not be changed retrospectively without a documented assessment.
- **Consent records:** Keep who, when, what they were told and how they consented. Do not use pre-ticked boxes. Provide a simple withdrawal method.
- **Electronic marketing:** Comply with PECR in addition to UK GDPR. Check the consent/"soft opt-in" position before sending marketing emails or texts, and honour opt-outs promptly. Marketing approach: [[PLACEHOLDER]]
- **Children:** Where services are offered directly to children, assess age-appropriate design, consent rules and the ICO Children's code. [[PLACEHOLDER: applicable? Y/N]]

## 6. Transparency

- Provide clear, concise privacy information at or before collection, and within one month where data is obtained from other sources.
- Privacy notices should cover:
  - identity and contact details, and the DPO if any
  - purposes and lawful bases
  - legitimate interests relied on, where applicable
  - categories of data, if obtained indirectly
  - recipients
  - international transfers and safeguards
  - retention periods or criteria
  - individual rights, including withdrawal of consent
  - the right to complain to the ICO
  - whether providing the data is required
  - any automated decision-making
- Notices to be provided for: [[PLACEHOLDER: e.g. website, employees, volunteers, supporters, clients, job applicants, CCTV]].
- Update notices when processing changes. Use plain language suited to the audience.

## 7. Individual rights

Individuals have the following rights, subject to conditions and exemptions:

| Right | Summary |
|---|---|
| Be informed | Receive privacy information (section 6). |
| Access (DSAR) | Obtain a copy of their personal data and supplementary information. |
| Rectification | Have inaccurate or incomplete data corrected. |
| Erasure | Have data deleted in certain circumstances. |
| Restriction | Limit processing in certain circumstances. |
| Portability | Receive certain data in a structured, machine-readable format, where processing is based on consent or contract and is automated. |
| Object | Object to processing based on legitimate interests or public task, and to direct marketing (absolute right). |
| Automated decisions | Safeguards for solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. Check current rules following the 2025 Act. |

**Handling requests:**
1. Requests can be made verbally or in writing, to anyone in the organisation. Staff must forward them to [[PLACEHOLDER: contact]] **the same working day**.
2. Log the date received and verify identity only where reasonably necessary.
3. Respond **without undue delay and within one month**. This can be extended by up to two further months for complex or numerous requests, and the individual must be told. Check current ICO guidance on clock-stopping and search reasonableness. Do not charge a fee unless a request is manifestly unfounded or excessive (or for further copies), in line with ICO guidance.
4. Apply exemptions only after review by [[PLACEHOLDER: role]]. Redact third-party data where appropriate.
5. Record the outcome and reasons. Where refusing, tell the individual about the right to complain to the ICO and to seek a judicial remedy.
6. **Complaints:** Individuals may complain to the organisation about its data handling. Complaints will be acknowledged and handled via [[PLACEHOLDER: process/timescale; check requirements under the 2025 Act]].

## 8. Special category and criminal offence data

- **Special category data:** race or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data (for unique identification), health, sex life or sexual orientation.
- **Criminal offence data** (Art. 10) is also subject to extra controls.
- Processing requires **both** an Art. 6 lawful basis **and** an Art. 9 condition. Criminal offence data requires an Art. 6 basis and a DPA 2018 Schedule 1 condition or official authority.
- Many conditions require a further DPA 2018 Schedule 1 condition. Some of these (such as employment, substantial public interest conditions) require an **appropriate policy document**. [[PLACEHOLDER: confirm whether required; if so, prepare separately or attach]]
- Record the conditions relied on in the ROPA. Special category data we expect to process: [[PLACEHOLDER: e.g. staff health records, equality monitoring, DBS checks]].
- Restrict access to those who need it, apply stronger security, and consider a DPIA (section 16).

## 9. Data minimisation

- Collect only the data needed for the stated purpose. Do not collect "just in case".
- Review forms, systems and spreadsheets for unnecessary fields before launch and at each policy review.
- Use anonymisation or pseudonymisation where possible. Note that pseudonymised data remains personal data.
- Limit internal access on a need-to-know basis. Avoid copying data into personal devices, email attachments or unmanaged tools.

## 10. Accuracy

- Take reasonable steps to ensure data is accurate and, where necessary, up to date. Correct or delete inaccurate data without delay.
- Verify key details at collection. Prompt periodic confirmation for [[PLACEHOLDER: e.g. staff, members, clients]] at least [[PLACEHOLDER: frequency]].
- Record opinions as opinions and keep factual notes clear. Where accuracy is disputed, note the dispute and consider restriction.
- Pass corrections to recipients of the data where feasible.

## 13. Processors and data sharing

- A **processor** handles personal data on the organisation's documented instructions (e.g. payroll, IT support, cloud, mailing services).
- Before engaging a processor: carry out proportionate due diligence on its security and data protection, and confirm the roles (controller, joint controller, processor).
- A **written contract** meeting UK GDPR Art. 28 must be in place. It should cover subject matter, duration, nature and purpose, data types, confidentiality, security, sub-processors, assistance with rights and breaches, deletion or return at the end, and audit or information rights.
- Keep a processor register: [[PLACEHOLDER: location/owner]]. Review contracts at least [[PLACEHOLDER: frequency]].
- **Sharing with other controllers** (e.g. partners, funders, regulators): confirm a lawful basis, share only what is necessary, and consider a data sharing agreement in line with the ICO Data Sharing Code of Practice. Document the decision.

## 14. International transfers

- A restricted transfer occurs when personal data is sent or made accessible to a recipient outside the UK (including via cloud services, support access or group companies).
- Before any transfer, identify it in the ROPA and confirm the transfer mechanism, in line with current ICO guidance:
  - UK adequacy regulations (including UK-approved arrangements for particular countries)
  - appropriate safeguards, such as the ICO's International Data Transfer Agreement (IDTA) or the UK Addendum to EU Standard Contractual Clauses, or binding corporate rules
  - a narrowly applied exception (derogation) in limited cases
- Document a transfer risk assessment where relying on appropriate safeguards. Check the current test and requirements following the 2025 Act.
- Update privacy notices to describe transfers. Known transfers: [[PLACEHOLDER: supplier, country, mechanism]]
- Staff must not use tools or services that store data outside the UK without approval from [[PLACEHOLDER: role]].

## 15. Personal data breaches

A personal data breach is a security incident leading to accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data (e.g. misdirected email, lost device, ransomware, unauthorised access).

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1. Report | Anyone who discovers or suspects a breach must report it **immediately** to [[PLACEHOLDER: contact/out-of-hours]]. Do not delay to investigate. |
| 2. Contain | Take immediate steps to limit harm (recover data, revoke access, isolate systems). |
| 3. Assess | The data protection lead assesses risk to individuals (type and volume of data, sensitivity, ease of identification, consequences). |
| 4. Notify the ICO | If the breach is **likely to result in a risk to individuals' rights and freedoms**, report to the ICO **without undue delay and within 72 hours of becoming aware**, with reasons for any delay. |
| 5. Notify individuals | If the risk is **likely to be high**, inform affected individuals without undue delay, in clear language, with advice on protective steps. |
| 6. Inform others | Consider insurers, police, banks, processors, and other regulators (e.g. [[PLACEHOLDER: Charity Commission, FCA, sector regulator]]). |
| 7. Record | Log **all** breaches, including those not reported, with facts, effects, decisions and actions. Log location: [[PLACEHOLDER]]. |
| 8. Review | Identify root causes and corrective actions. Report to [[PLACEHOLDER: governing body]]. |

Processors must notify the organisation of breaches without undue delay (contractually required).

## 16. Data protection impact assessments (DPIAs)

- A DPIA is **mandatory** before processing that is likely to result in a high risk to individuals. This includes systematic and extensive profiling with significant effects, large-scale special category or criminal offence data, and systematic monitoring of public areas. Follow the ICO's list of processing operations that require a DPIA.
- Consider a DPIA for new technologies, large-scale or
 sensitive processing, use of children's or other vulnerable people's data, data matching or combining datasets, biometrics, CCTV or other monitoring, and the introduction of AI or automated decision tools.
- **Process:**
  1. Describe the processing: nature, scope, context and purposes.
  2. Assess necessity and proportionality.
  3. Identify risks to individuals and the measures to reduce them.
  4. Record the advice of the data protection lead or DPO, and the decision-maker's sign-off on residual risk: [[PLACEHOLDER: role]].
  5. Where high residual risk remains that cannot be mitigated, consult the ICO **before** starting the processing.
  6. Review the DPIA when the processing changes.
- DPIAs should be started at the project design stage, before procurement or launch. DPIA register and template: [[PLACEHOLDER: location]]
